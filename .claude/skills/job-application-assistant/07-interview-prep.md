## STAR Format

Structure answers as: **Situation** (context), **Task** (your responsibility), **Action** (what you did), **Result** (outcome).

Keep answers to 1-2 minutes. Be specific. End with what you learned or would do differently.

### 1. Nine-box AI enablement framework (initiative, judgment, change management)
**S:** JY Construction had AI tools available but uneven adoption. Some people used them daily, most didn't, and generic training didn't move anyone — the beginners were overwhelmed and the confident users were bored.
**T:** As AI Enablement Specialist, get the tools actually used across the business rather than just deployed.
**A:** Rather than run more training, I mapped every user on two axes: their **access level** (limited, standardized, advanced) and their **AI proficiency** (beginner, medium, high). That gave nine cells, and each cell got a different intervention — basic written instructions, standardized templates, recorded demonstrations, group training, one-to-one coaching, or advanced workflow access. I gathered use cases department by department, and prioritized recurring complaints that affected several people over one-off requests.
**R:** Enablement became targeted instead of broadcast — beginners got structure, advanced users got access. I now measure success on adoption and repeat usage, whether people still use a workflow after training, and how many correction cycles an output needs — not on how many tools we've rolled out.
**Use for:** "Tell me about a time you took initiative", "Describe a time you solved a problem creatively", "How do you drive adoption of something new?", "Tell me about a framework or process you designed"
> *Add the concrete numbers before your interview: how many people, across how many departments, and what adoption looked like before and after.*

### 2. Peppa Pets Strata approval (persistence, stakeholder escalation)
**S:** A Vancouver commercial fit-out needed Strata approval covering added floor drains, penetrations, suspended drainage, electrical requirements, HVAC, fixture loads and bylaw compliance. The approval process stalled, and the whole project sat behind it.
**T:** Get the Strata approval letter moving without escalating into a dispute with the building.
**A:** I assembled the technical information the Strata actually needed rather than resending the original request, and when the process kept sitting, I contacted the property-management stakeholders directly instead of waiting on the existing chain.
**R:** The required response came back in roughly a week. I learned that a stalled approval is usually an information problem or a routing problem, and that identifying which one it is beats sending polite follow-ups.
**Use for:** "Tell me about a time you dealt with a difficult stakeholder", "How do you handle being blocked?", "Describe a time you had to be persistent", "Tell me about working with people outside your organization"
> *Note the boundary: I coordinated the approval and its technical information. I did not do the engineering design.*

### 3. Haier Biomedical inventory and scheduling visibility (analytical rigour, measurable impact)
**S:** As an AI & Business Operations intern across Haier Biomedical's Vancouver and Qingdao operations, inventory, warehouse output, overseas orders, customer requirements and shipments lived in separate places. Conflicts only surfaced once they had already affected execution.
**T:** Support the Logistics Lead by making the position visible early enough to act on.
**A:** I consolidated the data across SAP-style internal systems and Excel, compared planned against actual warehouse output, connected stock position to projected demand across a roughly six-week planning horizon, and standardized the weekly reporting so the same picture reached every team. I coordinated across a significant time-zone gap between Vancouver and Qingdao.
**R:** Four warehousing conflicts were caught before they hit execution, errors in three overseas schedules were corrected, and reporting was standardized for three functional groups.
**Use for:** "Tell me about a time you used data to make a decision", "Describe a measurable impact you had", "How do you work across time zones or cultures?", "Tell me about improving a process"

### 4. Victoria multi-project procurement (commercial judgment, working across projects)
**S:** I was running RFQs for two separate Victoria projects at once — Burger King #8515 at Tillicum and New York Fries #3302 at Hillside Centre — across painting, millwork, electrical, plumbing and gas, fire suppression, demolition, framing and drywall, flooring and scanning.
**T:** Get comparable, decision-ready pricing for both, from the mainland.
**A:** I issued separate RFQs per trade and pushed for the things that actually make bids comparable: scope clarification, exclusions, lead times, site-readiness requirements, and out-of-scope and change-order language. Because both projects sat on Vancouver Island, I compared travel, accommodation and ferry assumptions across them to identify where combining trips would take real cost out.
**R:** Bids could be compared on scope rather than headline price, and shared travel and mobilization became visible as a saving instead of being priced twice.
**Use for:** "Tell me about managing competing priorities", "Describe a time you saved money or found an efficiency", "How do you evaluate vendors?", "Tell me about a detail others missed"
> *Confirm the realized saving before quoting a figure — see `VERIFY-BEFORE-SUBMITTING.md`.*

### 5. UBC Chinese Debate Club tournament (leadership, logistics under deadline)
**S:** The club needed to run a full debate tournament — 40+ competitors and roughly 140 attendees — on a student budget and a fixed date.
**T:** As President / Event Coordinator, own the event end to end.
**A:** I coordinated venue, scheduling, volunteers, participants, communications and event-day logistics, and ran five matches for an individual championship. I also prepared proposals connected to national-level Canadian debate competitions.
**R:** The tournament ran to schedule at full attendance. It's the clearest example I have of owning something outright rather than coordinating it.
**Use for:** "Tell me about a time you led a team", "Describe your leadership style", "Tell me about an event or project you organized", "How do you delegate?"

## Common Tough Questions

### "Why are you leaving JY Construction so soon?" (expect this one — prepare it properly)
> Draft: "I joined in May and was promoted to Associate Project Manager five weeks later, so I've had a lot handed to me quickly and I've delivered on it. The part of the job I've built out furthest is the AI enablement work — the workflow environment, the knowledge base, the enablement framework — and that's a side responsibility there rather than the job. I want it to be the job."
>
> **Rules:** never criticize the employer; the "small construction company" deal-breaker is a *personal* direction decision, not a complaint, and should never be voiced as one. Lead with the promotion, not the tenure. If they press on the three months, answer it directly rather than deflecting — evasion is what makes short tenure look bad.

### "You don't have [specific skill]." — usually SQL depth, BI, or engineering
> Draft: "That's fair — my SQL is functional rather than deep, and I'd rather tell you that than find out in week two. What I do have is the layer above it: I built a 143-record knowledge base with 18 controlled fields specifically so that retrieval and routing would work, which meant thinking hard about structure and validation. I'm building the query depth underneath that deliberately, and here's what I'm working through right now."
>
> **Rules:** acknowledge the gap in the first sentence — bridging before acknowledging reads as dodging. Then name something concrete you're doing about it. Never claim advanced SQL, ML engineering, RAG engineering or production integration; those are explicit guardrails in CLAUDE.md and an interviewer will find the edge fast.

### "Where do you see yourself in 5 years?"
> Draft: "Owning AI implementation end to end — not just building workflows, but the strategy, rollout, training and governance around them, and being accountable for whether adoption actually holds. I've done a small version of that already; I want to do it at real scale, with the technical depth underneath it that I'm still building."

### "What's your biggest weakness?"
> Draft: "My range has outgrown my depth. I can coordinate twenty projects, build workflows, run enablement — but the technical foundation under the data work is intermediate, and I've been leaning on breadth to cover that. I'm addressing it deliberately rather than hoping nobody notices."
>
> **Rules:** this is a real weakness with a real mitigation, which is the point. Don't substitute a humblebrag ("I care too much"), and don't offer a weakness that disqualifies you for the specific role — if the job *is* data engineering, this is the wrong one to give, and the honest conclusion is that it's the wrong job.

### "Do you require sponsorship to work in Canada?"
> **No.** Work permit valid to 2029-07-08. Answer plainly and move on — do not volunteer permit details, expiry dates, or immigration plans unprompted.

### "Why this company specifically?"
> Customize per company. Must reference: specific projects, company values, market position, or team structure. Never give a generic answer.

## Questions You Should Ask Interviewers

### About the Role
- "What does a typical week look like in this role?"
- "What would success look like in the first 6 months?"
- "What's the biggest challenge the team is facing right now?"

### About the Team
- "How big is the team, and how do you divide work?"
- "What does the development/project lifecycle look like, from idea to production?"
- "How do you onboard new team members?"

### About Tech & Growth
- "What's your current tech stack for [relevant area]?"
- "Is there room to grow into more architectural or strategic decisions?"
- "How does the team stay current with new tools and methods?"

### About Culture (use these to prevent disappointment)
- "How would you describe the team culture?"
- "What does professional development look like here?"
- "Is there flexibility for remote/hybrid work?"
- "What's the balance between development/new projects and maintenance work?"
- "How would you describe the leadership style in this team?"
- "What do people who thrive here have in common?"

## Phone/Video Interview Tips
- Have STAR examples written out (use this file)
- Keep a glass of water nearby
- Smile when speaking (it changes your tone)
- Ask for clarification if a question is vague
- It's OK to take 5 seconds to think before answering
- End with: "Is there anything else you'd like to know about my background?"

## After the Application (Best Practice)

### Follow-Up Etiquette
- **Don't call to "stand out"** or to learn more about the role post-submission - this risks a negative impression
- If the employer specified a timeline, respect it and wait
- If no timeline was given and significant time has passed (2+ weeks), a brief call to ask about status is acceptable
- If you have genuinely new, relevant information to share, a short follow-up is fine

### Thank-You Notes
- When you receive any update (interview invitation, rejection, or status update), send a brief thank-you message
- Express appreciation for their time and the process
- Keep it short (2-3 sentences)

## Roleplay Guidelines
When the user asks for interview practice:
1. Ask which role/company to simulate
2. Start with easy warm-up questions ("Tell me about yourself")
3. Progress to role-specific technical questions
4. Include 1-2 behavioral questions using the competencies from the job posting
5. End with a tough question or curveball
6. After each answer, give brief feedback: what worked, what to sharpen
7. Suggest which STAR example would work best for each question
